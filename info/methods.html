THE DAMS

The American Rivers Dam Removal Database (March 2026) has 333 rows for California, Oregon and Washington.

  • One row is a duplicate. CA-091 repeats CA-148: both are TCFD11 on Trabuco Creek, same height, same barrier ID, 12 m apart. This leaves the count at 332 removals.
  • These 332 removals sit at 320 places. 22 rows fall in 10 clusters that the national barrier inventory records as one barrier each. The map still shows every dam in a cluster, but fish habitat is matched by distance, and at that range the dams are effectively in the same spot. So every dam in a cluster gets the same result. Those matching results come from a shared location, and don't count as separate confirmations.

276 dams are placed from American Rivers' own coordinates. American Rivers left 52 rows blank, and 12 of those were recovered by linking to the National Aquatic Barrier Inventory (NACC). 2 coordinates were corrected, each on cited evidence. CA-133 Lagunita Diversion, for example, was recorded beside the lake it once fed, 1.24 km from the creek the dam actually stood on. 2 more are voided: CA-157 and CA-158, the Silverado Creek fish dams, carry coordinates copied from two San Juan Creek dams 24.6 km away in another basin. They still count as removals but can't be drawn.

That leaves 290 on the map and 42 that no source can place. 18 of the 42 are California dams removed between 1925 and 1951, several on forks of the Trinity, Salmon and Scott. The search for their coordinates is closed. Historical topographic maps are the only thing left that could place them.

A coordinate can be accurate and still be stored too coarsely to use. 10 mapped dams are written to so few decimal places that the stored number can't express a position to within 75 m, which is the tolerance threshold used for this project. 113 more are stored to about ±56 m. Their results are reported like the rest, but read them with that in mind.

SPECIES LAYERS

The map includes 27 native fish species. For 10 of them, both layers below exist. 11 only have a presence layer and 6 only have a critical habitat layer.

ESA critical habitat

Federally designated critical habitat, from NMFS (Chinook, coho, steelhead, chum, sockeye, green sturgeon, eulachon) and USFWS (bull trout, Lost River sucker, shortnose sucker, Warner sucker, Santa Ana sucker, Little Kern golden trout, delta smelt, tidewater goby, Owens tui chub). One scope rule decided which USFWS species to include: fish only, where barrier passage is how a dam affects them. Frogs and amphibians with critical habitat in the region are out on that principle, not case by case.

One layer was rebuilt by this project. NMFS has never published a map of critical habitat for Southern Oregon / Northern California Coast (SONCC) coho, though the designation is law. The regulation (50 CFR 226.210) defines it in words: reaches accessible to coho, inside listed watersheds and listed counties, between Cape Blanco and Punta Gorda, below eleven named dams. Each clause became one step: fish distribution (ODFW in Oregon, CDFW in California), clipped to the listed watersheds and counties, trimmed to the ESU's own range, minus the basins above the boundary dams. It's a reconstruction, not an official layer, and the map draws it in a distinct shade of the coho color for that reason.

It should be noted that the regulation lists Jackson County, Oregon, for the upper Klamath but not Klamath County, which is where John C. Boyle Dam stood. So John C. Boyle reads "no critical habitat" while Chinook, coho and steelhead are all recorded present at it.

Presence

The presence layers show where state and federal surveys have recorded 21 native migratory species. Each state has its own sources: ODFW Fish Habitat Distribution in Oregon, WDFW SWIFD in Washington, and CDFW and NOAA distribution layers in California. USFWS Pacific lamprey data covers all three states, and where California has nothing finer, PISCES watershed ranges fill the gap.

Each source records presence differently, so every source was filtered by the same five principles, written down before any code ran:

  1. Presence needs evidence, not a model. A record of the fish, or an expert's presumption, counts. A prediction from slope or habitat suitability doesn't. (This drops WDFW's large "Gradient Accessible" class, which is a model of where fish could reach.)
  2. Reintroduced populations of a native species count. Reintroduction is often why a dam comes out.
  3. Fish trucked past a barrier are present.
  4. Use the most detailed data available. Where a source maps the fish stream by stream, those lines are used. Whole-watershed ranges are used only where no stream-level data exists.
  5. Exclude only what the source marks as resident-only. Records that don't say either way stay in. Washington, for example, lists 85% of its cutthroat as "Unknown". Dropping those would reflect how the records were kept, not where the fish actually are.

Also fixed: anything marked historical is out, and a state agency's records are used only inside its own state.

One exception, and its cost: Washington has no "redband trout" label. Redband is inferred from rainbow trout east of the Cascades, and principle 5 doesn't apply to it, so only fluvial or adfluvial lines count. That leaves out 157 lines where no life history was recorded, and every Washington redband match is graded inferred (see below).

Four species have no Washington source at all: western river lamprey, largescale sucker, bridgelip sucker and eulachon. A blank at a Washington dam for those four means no source exists, not that the fish is absent. (Eulachon still reaches Washington dams through its critical habitat.)

SPATIAL JOIN

Each of the 290 dams was checked against the full-resolution source data, never the simplified copies drawn on screen.

The base tolerance is 75 m. A dam matches a habitat or distribution line within 75 m. That number isn't tuned to a result. It's the top of an error budget:

  • the same dam in two databases differs by 40 m at the 75th percentile (measured on 263 pairs)
  • streams are mapped to about 12 m
  • a stream line runs down the channel's center while a dam point can sit at one abutment, up to about 30 m away
  • different agencies draw the same stream tens of meters apart.

Independent errors combine to about 51 m, and adding them straight gives about 82 m. They aren't fully independent (a poorly recorded dam tends to sit on a poorly mapped stream), so 75 m sits between the two.

Same-stream extension, up to 500 m. A dam can also match a line up to 500 m away, but only if the line's stream name is exactly the same as the river recorded for the dam. This is how Iron Gate Dam matches coho: the coho line is 95 m away, on the Klamath River. A line labeled "Tributary to" a river doesn't count as that river. NMFS and USFWS layers don't include stream names, so they only use the 75 m tolerance.

The extension stops at a closer channel. Sometimes a dam sits within 75 m of a mapped channel, named or unnamed, that isn't the river it's recorded on. In that case the dam is treated as being on the closer channel. The extension can't reach past it to pick up species from the main stem, the larger river further away. This happened at six dams. The rule was chosen on purpose, because it's better to understate what a removal did than to overstate it.

Watershed ranges (PISCES, California) match by containment, with the same 75 m allowance. A watershed's boundary crosses the main stem at its outlet, the point where it drains into the next watershed downstream. Dams are often built at exactly that point. A dam there sits almost on the boundary line, so a plain inside-or-outside test can go either way from a small error in the dam's position. Iron Gate sits 0.24 m outside the Chinook range and 0.24 m inside all three Klamath sucker ranges.

Results

Of the 290 mapped dams:

  • 144 sit on ESA critical habitat, about half of the map.
  • 187 have at least one species recorded present, about two thirds.
  • 141 have both. All but 3 of the dams on critical habitat also have a recorded species.
  • 100 have neither, about a third. 68 of those are small structures on a handful of Orange County creeks.

Critical habitat and presence are kept in separate fields and never merged. They're different claims by different agencies, and where they disagree (as at John C. Boyle, or at Condit's chum salmon), the disagreement is itself a result.

VERIFICATION OF RESULTS

  • Against an independent source: The national barrier inventory (NACC) also records fish habitat at each barrier. Across 10 species and 1,170 dam/species pairs, it agrees with this project 85% of the time. Most of the disagreement, 140 pairs, comes from the two asking different questions: NACC counts miles of habitat anywhere in a barrier's river network, while this project asks what is at the dam. 20 pairs were real disagreements. Each was traced to a stated rule above (most often the exclusion of historical or modeled habitat) or to the tolerance. None of them revealed an error in the matching. The full comparison is in nacc_crosscheck.csv.
  • Against the national stream map: Where a dam matched nothing, or matched only through the same-stream extension, the stream right at the dam was looked up in the USGS National Hydrography Dataset. Dams whose answer was already known went through the same check, as controls. The results are in nhd_channel_names.csv.
  • Every blank is explained. A dam can be blank for a species for four reasons: the fish isn't mapped there, a filter rule excluded it, it's just past the tolerance, or no source exists for that species in that state. An audit script, audit_join_gaps.R, reports which reason applies for each dam and species, in join_gap_audit.csv.

LIMITATIONS

  • The states map presence differently. Oregon and Washington map stream reaches, while California often maps whole watersheds. A species can look far more widespread in California just because the mapping unit is bigger.
  • Some California layers understate, and some overstate. CDFW's coho layer is built from sightings. It traces each sighting since 1990 downstream to the ocean and includes nothing above the highest sighting, so it understates. PISCES watershed ranges overstate. A dam on a tributary inside a range reads "present" even if the fish never use that tributary.
  • Sources span 2005 to 2026. California Chinook alone combines stream lines from 2005 with watershed ranges from 2014.
  • Datum and distance don't change any result. Some sources use the NAD83 datum and some use WGS84. A datum is the reference model of the earth that coordinates are measured against, and here the two differ by about 1.5 m. Measuring on a flat projection instead of the curved earth changes a 75 m distance by less than 1 m.
  • Layers may appear close to dams, but not actually be matches. The layers are drawn simplified. A dam's matches are listed in its popup, and a line that looks close to it may not be one of them.

TECHNICAL

  • Albers equal-area conic projection, with standard parallels at 34°N and 48°N, the two latitudes where distances are true to scale. Web tile maps are locked to Web Mercator, which stretches the map more the farther north it goes. Across this map's extent (33.6°N to 48.9°N), Mercator would stretch Washington 1.27× relative to Southern California, so it would read about 27% larger than it should.
  • Display shapes are simplified to about 33 m (0.0003°), and are never used for a match or a measurement. Fish data lying entirely in Idaho, Montana or Nevada is left off. Anything touching the three states, or within 3 km of them, stays.
  • Shaded relief switches from a 500 m elevation model to a 115 m one as you zoom in. Relief and lakes are clipped to the three states.
  • Overlapping species cover each other. Two species on the same reach can't both be drawn there, so the one later in the list covers the earlier one. Colors are ordered so species that share water never get neighboring colors, which keeps the overlap visible. The species switches can show each one on its own.
  • Nothing is loaded from another server. There's no basemap, tile service, API key, analytics, web fonts or CDN (a third-party file host). Every file, including the one library (D3 v7.9.0), is served with the page.
